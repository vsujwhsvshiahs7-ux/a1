Mastering Traditional Hand Anglage & Black Polish

Deconstructing 45-degree hand-filed bridge chamfers, inward sharp anglage corners, gentian wood peg burnishing, and optical black polish techniques.

In the rarefied world of haute horology, what separates an ordinary mechanical movement from an artistic masterpiece is not merely its chronometric accuracy, but the soul bestowed upon its components through traditional hand finishing. While modern CNC milling and spark erosion machines can cut metal with microscopic precision, no automated machine can replicate the fluid radiance of hand-filed anglage.

The practice of Hand Anglage (Chamfering) & Specular Black Polish represents the pinnacle of watchmaking craftsmanship. At Mechanic Time Ox, our finishers spend upwards of eighty hours hand-beveling a single movement’s bridges, levers, and screw heads. In this masterclass, we explore the sacred tools, abrasive physics, and optical perfection of traditional Swiss and Saxon hand finishing.

1. The Art of Hand Anglage: 45-Degree Chamfers

How master watchmakers transform raw stamped edges into mirror ribbons:

  • Hand-Filing the Initial Bevel: Using hardened steel degussit files, the artisan carefully cuts a uniform 45-degree angle along the perimeter of German silver or brass bridges.
  • The Inward Corner Challenge (*Angles Rentrants*): While automated machines can create rounded external corners, a razor-sharp inward corner where two bevels meet at a single point can only be executed by human hand.
  • Gentian Wood Peg Burnishing: Final mirror luster is achieved by rubbing the chamfer with dried stems of alpine gentian wood charged with diamond lapping paste down to 0.5-micron grit.

“An inward sharp anglage corner is the signature of human dedication; it is an optical declaration that machine automation stopped and the hand of a master took over.”

2. Specular Black Polish (*Poli Noir* / Block Polish)

The supreme optical finish where steel turns from blinding mirror to pitch black:

  1. Zinc Lapping Plates: The steel component (such as a tourbillon bridge or swan-neck regulator) is affixed to a wooden peg and rubbed in figure-eight patterns across a flat zinc plate coated with diamond paste.
  2. Absolute Flatness: The surface must achieve optical flatness down to a fraction of a light wavelength with zero rounded edges.
  3. The Black Reflection Phenomenon: When viewed perpendicular to light, the surface reflects 100% of rays directly into the eye like liquid mercury; when tilted slightly, it appears deep obsidian black.

3. Côtes de Genève, Perlage & Sinks

Harmonizing contrasting decorative textures across the caliber:

Our movements combine linear Côtes de Genève waves, circular overlapping perlage graining on the mainplate, and mirror-polished screw countersinks to create breathtaking visual depth.

5. Untreated German Silver (*Maillechort*) Mainplates

We craft our bridges from untreated German silver (an alloy of copper, zinc, and nickel), which develops a warm, golden-amber patina over decades of connoisseur ownership.

6. Flame-Blued Steel Screws and Polished Sinks

Steel screw heads are flat-polished to a mirror finish and heated over a brass shavings pan to precisely 590°F (310°C), forming a protective cornflower-blue titanium oxide film.

7. Microscopic Inspection Under 20x Magnification

Every finished component is audited under stereo microscopes to ensure zero file marks, microscopic burrs, or uneven bevel widths before final movement assembly.

9. Natural Elderberry Pith Cleaning of Polished Bridges

10. Traditional Boxwood Buffing for Concave Profiles

Curved bridge flanks are hand-rubbed using custom-carved boxwood laps impregnated with alumina powders, yielding uniform satin-brushed striations that contrast against polished bevels.

12. Diamond Lapping Film Gradients on Zinc Blocks

Achieving flawless black mirror polish requires progressive lapping across diamond films starting at 6-micron grit and descending through 3-micron, 1-micron, and 0.25-micron steps, creating an optically flat surface that reflects light like liquid mercury.

Our finishers harvest dried stems of wild alpine gentian wood, carving custom wedge tips charged with diamond paste to hand-burnish 45-degree bridge bevels to a flawless, distortion-free mirror sheen under high magnification.

Frequently Asked Questions on Hand Anglage

How can I tell if anglage was done by hand or machine?+
Look closely at the inward corners (where two bevels meet). A machine can only produce rounded internal corners, while hand finishing creates a razor-sharp, seamless point of intersection.
What is black polish and why is it so difficult?+
Black polish requires lapping a steel part on a zinc plate until it is perfectly flat down to light wavelengths. Any slight hand tilt or dust particle creates microscopic scratches, requiring the artisan to restart.
